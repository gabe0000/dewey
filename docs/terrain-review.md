## Lessons and examples

1. **Ridges, valleys, saddles & reentrants** uses labeled original contour schematics to distinguish raised ground and hollows. Elevation evidence and surrounding patterns take precedence over a letter-shaped outline.
2. **Match contours to the ground** compares equal contour intervals and map scales, distinguishes observations from inferences, and uses stationary observations or paper profiles.
3. **Estimate position from known features** starts with known mapped points and possible route stretches. A fictional true-bearing paper example reverses sightings of 045° and 315° into back bearings of 225° and 135°. The overlap is uncertain and must be checked against independently identified features and terrain. It is not a walking instruction.
4. **Stop, check & relocate in practice** rehearses an evidence mismatch while the helper already knows the physical location. Recovering the correct map position can happen without moving. A confirmed safe return on a known route is distinct from an uncertain route requiring stopping and help.

Every lesson has equipment, goals, an original worked example and drill, four answer checks, a prominent practice boundary, source-page links and prerequisite links. ## Practice and uncertainty

Begin with paper or a model. Outdoor work stays in daylight, suitable conditions, familiar permitted areas and safe boundaries with a helper who remains with the learners. Do not create a real lost-person drill, separate participants, blindfold learners, or send them searching for a missing feature. A terrain label or plotted overlap does not establish route safety or position certainty. Real emergencies use the emergency plan and appropriate help, with no wandering toward a guessed landmark. The material is not field-tested, a rescue plan or an independent-navigation qualification.
